How I work

Some emotions and experiences are hard to speak, see, and feel. Deep listening means getting involved in how life is right now, and how it has always been, or felt. I really want to know how you experience your life.

My approach draws on psychodynamics, attachment, and your history of developmental and experiential trauma, alongside your culture, identity, and childhood. The process can be vulnerable, frustrating, and liberating. Connection, acceptance, and empathy bring peace of mind.

My psychoanalytic curiosity often turns to the impact of early, pre-verbal life disturbances, helping you connect your suffering to challenging body experiences or intense life events. Hard-to-describe struggles in relationships, cultural identity, career, or life transitions can bring up conflicts from the past.

Therapy is an intimate, organic process that takes time. We usually begin weekly and adjust as we learn what you need. If you're new to this, I encourage you to meet several therapists and find the connection that feels right.

Common concerns I work with: anxiety · depression · trauma · ADHD & neurodivergence · relationship issues & family conflict · racial & cultural identity · life transitions · LGBTQ+ · caregivers · chronic pain · addiction. I also offer consultation for psychotherapists and for those already in therapy.

Common questions

What is psychoanalytic and psychodynamic therapy, and how is it different from CBT?

Psychoanalytic and psychodynamic therapy considers the patterns that happen that often are outside of our conscious awareness. Rather than focusing on our ideas, thoughts, cognitions of our lives and teaching techniques to manage symptoms, we try to understand what those symptoms are expressing and where they come from. It tends to be more open-ended and can be experienced as depth-oriented since it often draws from our oldest experiences of our lives rather than recent short-term, skills-based approaches like CBT which draw upon our ideas about our lives. Many people come to this work because quicker fixes haven't reached what actually troubles them.

Is psychoanalytic and psychodynamic therapy evidence-based?

Yes. Psychodynamic psychotherapy has been studied in controlled trials and meta-analyses, and it performs about as well as other established treatments, including CBT, with gains that tend to hold or even grow after therapy ends. Two honest qualifications, though. The strongest evidence is for time-limited, weekly psychodynamic therapy; for intensive, long-term analysis specifically, the controlled research is thinner and still developing. And the research does not show that any single approach is right for a particular type of person: fit, your own engagement, and the relationship between us predict outcomes more reliably than the name of the method. That is why I encourage anyone new to therapy to meet a few clinicians and choose the connection that feels right, rather than the label.

Sources: Steinert et al., Am J Psychiatry, 2017 · Abbass et al., Cochrane Database Syst Rev, 2014

Is a Licensed Clinical Social Worker (LCSW) the same as a psychologist?

No. They're different licenses. In New York, "psychologist" is a protected title held by clinicians with a doctorate in psychology. In New York, "psychoanalyst" is also a protected term; internationally, through the IPA, those who complete psychoanalytic training can be considered psychoanalysts. I'm a Licensed Clinical Social Worker (LCSW), trained to provide psychotherapy, and a candidate in psychoanalytic training at IPTAR, so someday I will hold the psychoanalyst title, but for now I am a psychoanalytic candidate. I do psychoanalytically informed psychotherapy, not psychological testing or neuropsychological evaluations; if you specifically need testing, I'm glad to point you toward a colleague.
